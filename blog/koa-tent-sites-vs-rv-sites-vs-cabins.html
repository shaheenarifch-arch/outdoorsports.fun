Quick answer

At a KOA campground you can usually choose between a tent site, an RV site and a cabin, and the right pick comes down to what you already own, how much comfort you want and who is coming. Choose a tent site if you have a tent and want the classic, lowest-cost experience. Choose an RV site if you travel with a camper or trailer and need hookups. Choose a camping cabin if you want a roof and a real bed without buying gear, or a deluxe cabin if you want a bathroom and kitchen too. If you camp more than a couple of nights a year, joining KOA Rewards for 10% off stays usually pays for itself.

Key takeaways

  • KOA runs 500+ campgrounds across the U.S. and Canada, from highway stopovers to resort-style parks.
  • There are three campground styles: KOA Journey (convenient overnight stops), KOA Holiday (destination camping with more activities) and KOA Resort (the most amenities).
  • Site types vary by campground. Most offer tent sites and RV sites; many add camping cabins, deluxe cabins or glamping options.
  • Book popular dates early. Summer weekends, holidays and campgrounds near national parks fill first.
  • KOA Rewards saves 10% on stays for members, with an annual fee; check the current terms before you join.

Tent sites, RV sites and cabins explained

Tent sites

The classic camping experience. Tent sites usually include a flat pad, a fire ring or grill and a picnic table, with shared restrooms and showers nearby. Some campgrounds offer sites with water and electric hookups for tent campers, which is handy for charging lights or running a fan in summer. Tent sites are the lowest-cost option and the best way to get the full outdoors feel.

RV sites

RV sites come with combinations of water, electric and sewer hookups. Pull-through sites let you drive in and out without reversing, which is great for longer rigs and one-night stops. Back-in sites can be quieter and more private. Check the amp service (commonly 30 or 50 amp) and maximum length before you book.

Camping cabins

Camping cabins are simple wooden cabins with beds and electricity, but usually no bathroom or kitchen inside. You bring bedding, use the campground restrooms and cook outside on the grill. They are ideal for first-timers or for rainy-season trips when you do not want to pitch a tent.

Deluxe cabins and glamping

Deluxe cabins add a private bathroom and a kitchen or kitchenette, and often linens. Some locations also offer glamping tents, yurts or other unique stays. These cost more but feel closer to a small vacation home, while you still have campfires and camp activities outside the door.

If you are unsure, book one night in a cabin and one on a tent site on the same trip. It is a low-risk way to find out what your group enjoys most before you invest in more gear.

Prices vary widely by location, season and day of week, so compare the same dates across a few campgrounds before you book.

Decision framework: choose in five questions

  1. What do you already own? If you have a good tent, start there. If you own nothing, a cabin avoids a big gear bill.
  2. Who is coming? Toddlers, older relatives and first-time campers usually enjoy a cabin more. Teens and outdoor regulars often prefer tents.
  3. What will the weather be? Spring and autumn nights can be cold. A cabin or a site with electric for a heater makes shoulder-season trips much easier.
  4. How long are you staying? One night on the road: a Journey campground near the highway. Several nights: a Holiday or Resort with more to do.
  5. What is your budget per night? Tent sites are cheapest; deluxe cabins cost the most. Membership discounts help most on longer or repeat trips.

What to pack for each site type

Tent site essentials

  • Tent with footprint, stakes and a mallet
  • Sleeping bags rated for the night-time low, sleeping pads or air mattress
  • Headlamps and a camp lantern
  • Stove or grill tools, cookware, cooler, water jug
  • Camp chairs and a rain tarp

Camping cabin essentials

  • Bedding or sleeping bags and pillows (check whether the cabin provides any)
  • Shower kit and towels for the shared bathhouse
  • Cooking gear for the outdoor grill, plus a small cooler
  • Extension cord and power strip for charging
  • Slippers or camp shoes for night trips to the restroom

RV site essentials

  • Correct power adapter for your amp service and a surge protector
  • Fresh-water hose, water pressure regulator and sewer hose kit
  • Leveling blocks and wheel chocks
  • Outdoor mat and camp chairs

How to save money on KOA stays

  • Join KOA Rewards if you camp often. Members save 10% on stays and earn points toward future camping. There is an annual membership fee, so it pays off fastest for families who camp several nights a year. Check current terms on the KOA Rewards page.
  • Travel midweek. Weekday nights are often easier to book and can cost less than weekends.
  • Try shoulder season. Late spring and early autumn bring fewer crowds and pleasant days, though nights are cooler.
  • Book early for peak dates. Holiday weekends and campgrounds near popular parks fill months ahead.
  • Bring your own food. Cooking at camp is one of the biggest savings compared with eating out on a trip.
  • Read the cancellation policy. Policies vary by campground and season; know them before you pay.

Camping season by season

Spring

Spring brings green trails, wildflowers and fewer crowds, but also rain and cold nights. A cabin or a tent site with electric hookups for a small heater makes early-season trips more comfortable. Pack a rain tarp, waterproof boots and extra warm layers, and check whether seasonal campgrounds in colder regions have opened yet.

Summer

Peak season. Pools, splash pads and planned activities are in full swing at many Holiday and Resort campgrounds, and popular dates fill quickly, so book early. Choose a shaded site if you can, bring a fan for the tent or cabin, and plan hikes for early morning to avoid afternoon heat and storms.

Autumn

Many campers' favorite season: cooler days, changing leaves and quieter campgrounds after Labor Day. Some campgrounds host harvest or Halloween events. Nights can drop sharply, so a cabin or warmer sleeping bag pays off.

Winter

Some KOAs stay open year-round, especially in milder climates, while others close for the season. Winter camping in a deluxe cabin can be cozy and quiet. Confirm opening dates, water availability and road conditions before you go.

Camping with kids: make it easy

  • Start with one or two nights. Short first trips build excitement instead of exhaustion.
  • Choose a campground with things to do. Playgrounds, pools, jumping pillows and planned activities keep kids busy while adults set up.
  • Book a site near the restrooms. Night-time bathroom trips are much easier.
  • Give every child a headlamp. It makes the dark fun rather than scary.
  • Plan simple meals. Foil-packet dinners, hot dogs and s'mores are easy wins. Prep at home to cut work at camp.
  • Bring a rainy-day bag. Cards, books and a small game save a wet afternoon.

Campground etiquette

Good neighbors make campgrounds better for everyone:

  • Respect quiet hours. Keep music and voices down in the evening and early morning.
  • Stay on your site. Do not cut through other campers' sites.
  • Handle fire safely. Use the fire ring, never leave a fire unattended and put it out completely with water. Follow any burn bans.
  • Use local firewood. Moving firewood can spread pests. Buy it at or near the campground.
  • Keep pets leashed and clean up after them.
  • Leave no trace. Pack out trash, recycle where possible and leave the site cleaner than you found it.

Sample plan: a 3-day KOA weekend

Friday

Leave after lunch so you arrive in daylight. Check in at the office, pick up firewood and ice at the camp store, then set up. For a tent site, pitch the tent first, then the kitchen area and chairs. For a cabin, unload bedding and lanterns and you are done in minutes. Keep dinner simple, such as pre-made chili warmed on the grill, and enjoy the first campfire.

Saturday

Start with a slow camp breakfast. Spend the morning on a nearby trail, lake or attraction while it is cool, and come back for lunch and pool or playground time. Late afternoon is ideal for a short bike ride around the campground. Dinner could be foil-packet potatoes and sausages, followed by s'mores and stargazing. Check the campground's activity board; many hold weekend events.

Sunday

Pack slowly after breakfast. Shake out and dry the tent before folding it if you can, so it does not mildew at home. Do a final sweep of the site for trash and small items, check out on time and stop somewhere for lunch on the drive home.

Simple budget checklist

  • Two nights' site or cabin fees (minus your rewards discount if you are a member)
  • Firewood and ice
  • Groceries for five meals plus snacks
  • Fuel for the round trip
  • Any activity or attraction tickets nearby

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Booking the wrong campground type. A highway Journey stop is great for one night but may not have the activities you want for a week.
  • Assuming cabins include bedding. Many camping cabins do not. Check before you pack.
  • Ignoring RV length and amp limits. Confirm your rig fits the site and you have the right adapter.
  • Underestimating night temperatures. Pack for the forecast low, not the daytime high.
  • Arriving after check-in hours without warning. Call ahead if you will be late.
  • Forgetting pet rules. Most KOAs are pet-friendly, but rules on leashes, breeds and cabin access vary.

Limitations and checks

Amenities, site types, pet rules and prices differ between KOA locations and change by season. Always check the individual campground's page before you book. Fire restrictions can apply during dry periods, so follow posted rules and local guidance.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between KOA Journey, Holiday and Resort?

KOA Journey campgrounds sit near highways for convenient overnight stops. KOA Holiday campgrounds are destination parks with more recreation. KOA Resort campgrounds offer the most amenities and services.

Do KOA camping cabins have bathrooms?

Basic camping cabins usually do not have a private bathroom; you use the campground restrooms. Deluxe cabins usually include a bathroom and a kitchen or kitchenette. Check each listing.

Do I need to bring bedding to a KOA cabin?

For most camping cabins, yes: bring sleeping bags or bedding and pillows. Some deluxe cabins include linens. Check the cabin details before your trip.

Is KOA Rewards worth it?

Members save 10% on stays and earn points. Because there is an annual fee, it is most worthwhile if you camp several nights a year. Compare the fee with your expected savings.

Are KOA campgrounds pet-friendly?

Most KOA campgrounds welcome pets, but rules vary, including leash rules and whether pets are allowed in cabins. Check the campground's pet policy before booking.

Can I tent camp at every KOA?

Most KOAs offer tent sites, but not all. Use the search filters on KOA's website to find campgrounds with tent sites at your destination.
